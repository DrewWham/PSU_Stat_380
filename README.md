### Data Wrangling Exam

The beginning of the course will review and extend foundational R skills, RStudio workflows, data import, data manipulation, `data.table`, and related computational skills.

This opening portion of the course is intended to establish a common computational foundation for the statistical learning work that follows.

A Data Wrangling Exam will assess your proficiency with these foundational skills.

### Reading Quizzes

Reading quizzes will be assigned episodically when textbook chapters or other readings accompany the material being covered in class.

When a reading quiz is assigned, it will generally be due before the class in which that material is discussed. The purpose of the quizzes is to encourage students to encounter important concepts independently before we use class time to emphasize major ideas, work through applications, clarify difficult concepts, and connect the reading to computational problems.

### Projects

After the initial R and data-wrangling portion of the course, much of STAT 380 will be organized around a sequence of applied statistical learning projects.

Projects will generally involve a Kaggle competition running in the background while we study methods relevant to the problem in class. Lectures and demonstrations may use related or "sibling" problems rather than directly solving the competition problem for you.

Project work is intended to combine:

- statistical reasoning;
- computational problem solving;
- model development;
- validation and evaluation;
- interpretation;
- reproducible code; and
- the ability to explain and defend the analysis you produced.

Projects are not graded solely on whether you can generate a high-performing Kaggle submission.

### Project Performance Score

Each Kaggle competition will contain instructor-generated benchmark submissions representing solutions at varying levels of sophistication.

Benchmarks may correspond to performance scores such as:

- 70 points
- 75 points
- 80 points
- 85 points
- 90 points
- 100 points

Your **Project Performance Score** will be determined by the highest benchmark that your eligible private-leaderboard performance exceeds.

The intent is that the 100-point benchmark represents a problem that every student in the course should ultimately be capable of solving successfully. Reaching that benchmark demonstrates that you have produced code capable of solving the computational problem. It does **not**, by itself, establish that you understand the code or have met the project's learning objectives.

### Project Understanding Score

Each project will also receive a **Project Understanding Score**.

This score is intended to measure whether:

1. your submitted code satisfies the project-specific expectations provided in advance; and
2. you personally understand the analysis represented by that code.

The Project Understanding Score will generally have two approximately equal components.

#### Code and Rubric Review

Your submitted GitHub repository will be evaluated using a project-specific rubric that is available before the project deadline.

Rubric expectations will differ from project to project and may address areas such as:

- required statistical or computational methods;
- data preparation;
- validation procedures;
- model construction;
- reproducibility;
- organization and clarity of code; and
- other learning objectives specific to the project.

Meeting a Kaggle benchmark does not automatically establish that these requirements have been met.

#### Project Understanding Activity

During approximately the final 30 minutes of the first class meeting after a project is due, you will complete an in-class **Project Understanding Activity**.

You will bring a **printed copy of the code you submitted for the project**. You may consult your printed code while completing the activity, but you may not use a computer, phone, internet resource, AI system, or other outside assistance.

The questions will not be provided in advance.

Questions will frequently ask you to identify, describe, or explain elements of **your own submitted analysis**. Because students may solve projects differently, answers may differ from student to student.

For example, you might be asked to:

- identify the line of code specifying which predictor variables were used in a model;
- explain how your training and validation data were created;
- identify where a particular model was fit;
- explain the role of a tuning parameter;
- identify the code that generated your final predictions;
- explain a regularization choice; or
- describe why a particular modeling decision was made.

Your answers will be evaluated against the code you submitted to GitHub.

The purpose is **not to test whether you have memorized R syntax**. You will have your own code in front of you. The purpose is to determine whether you understand the computational and statistical analysis you submitted.

### Calculating the Project Grade

The final grade for each project will be calculated as:

$$
\text{Project Grade}
=
\text{Project Performance Score}
\times
\text{Project Understanding Score}
$$

For example, a Project Performance Score of 90% combined with a Project Understanding Score of 80% results in:

$$
0.90 \times 0.80 = 0.72
$$

or a **72% project grade**.

This multiplicative structure is intentional.

A successful statistical learning project requires both:

1. an analysis that performs successfully on unseen data; and
2. an understanding of the analysis that produced that performance.

Using sophisticated tools to create an excellent model does not satisfy the learning objectives of the course if you cannot explain or understand what you submitted.

### Project Understanding Forgiveness Policy

There will be approximately seven projects during the semester.

At the end of the semester, your **two lowest Project Understanding Scores among eligible non-capstone projects will automatically be replaced with 100%** when calculating those project grades.

This provides built-in flexibility for circumstances such as:

- missing the class meeting containing a Project Understanding Activity;
- forgetting to bring your printed code;
- having an unusually poor performance on one understanding activity; or
- other occasional circumstances that interfere with demonstrating your understanding on a particular project.

Students who complete every Project Understanding Activity will still benefit because their two lowest eligible understanding scores will be replaced with 100%.

**The Final Capstone Project is not eligible for this replacement.** Its Project Understanding Score will always be used when calculating the capstone grade.

### Final Capstone Project

The Final Capstone Project follows the same general philosophy as the other projects but is larger in scope and represents a more substantial integration of the skills developed throughout the semester.

It is worth **20% of the final course grade**.

The capstone will include both performance and understanding components, and its Project Understanding Score cannot be replaced under the forgiveness policy described above.

## Course Description and Objectives

### Description

The official course description is available in Penn State's University Bulletin [linked here](https://bulletins.psu.edu/search/?scontext=all&search=stat+380&caturl=%2Fundergraduate), but a recent version is reproduced below for your convenience.

A case study-based course in the use of computing and statistical reasoning to answer data-intensive questions. STAT 380 Data Science Through Statistical Reasoning and Computation (3) addresses the fact that real data are often messy by taking a holistic view of statistical analysis to answer questions of interest.

Various case studies lead students from the computationally intensive process of obtaining and cleaning data, through exploratory techniques, and finally to rudimentary inferential statistics. This process exploits students' exposure to introductory statistics as well as the R programming language, hence the required prerequisites, while novel computing and analytical techniques are introduced throughout the course.

For the collection of data, students learn scripting and database querying skills; for their exploration, they employ R capabilities for graphical and summary statistics; and for their analysis, they build upon the basic concepts obtained in their introductory statistics course. The varied case studies elucidate additional statistical topics such as identifying sources of bias and searching for high-dimensional outliers.

### Course Approach

In this offering of STAT 380, we will use computation to develop an applied understanding of statistical learning.

The semester begins by strengthening the R and data-management foundation developed in prerequisite coursework, with particular attention to RStudio, reproducible workflows, and `data.table`.

From there, the course uses applied projects to explore topics including:

- supervised learning;
- regression and classification;
- prediction on unseen data;
- probability-based predictions and model evaluation;
- regularization;
- cross-validation;
- ridge and lasso regression;
- tree-based ensemble methods;
- boosted models;
- dimensionality reduction;
- principal components analysis;
- nonlinear representations;
- unsupervised learning;
- clustering and mixture models; and
- high-dimensional and representation-based learning problems.

Throughout the semester, statistical concepts will be paired with computational applications. Kaggle projects provide an environment in which students can repeatedly develop models, submit predictions, receive feedback on unseen data, and improve their solutions.

The goal is not simply to learn a list of modeling packages. The course emphasizes understanding **why statistical learning methods work, how they generalize beyond observed data, how regularization and validation affect learning, and how computational methods allow statistical reasoning to operate on complex and high-dimensional problems**.

## AI-Assisted Grading

Penn State AI Studio may be used to assist with portions of the grading process for project-based work in STAT 380.

The primary use of AI will be **rubric verification of student-submitted code**. For example, AI Studio may be used to help identify whether a submission contains required project elements such as:

- use of relative file paths;
- an appropriate training/validation workflow;
- a required statistical or machine-learning method;
- reproducible code needed to generate project results;
- other objective requirements specified in the project rubric.

AI may also assist in locating the specific code or evidence relevant to a rubric criterion.

The instructor establishes all grading rubrics, determines the academic importance and point value of each criterion, reviews AI-generated findings and supporting evidence, and retains responsibility for verifying and assigning final grades. AI is used as a tool to increase the amount and consistency of project work that can be reviewed, not to independently determine student achievement.

This use of AI is intended to be **additive** to the grading process. It allows more aspects of student work and course learning objectives to be evaluated consistently than would otherwise be practical through manual code review alone.

If you believe that an AI-assisted factual determination about your submitted work is incorrect, you may request a **manual review by the instructor**. Students will not be penalized for requesting such a review.

Student work used in this process will be handled through Penn State-approved systems and in accordance with applicable University policies.
